Every bank goes down. What regulators now judge is what you did in the first hour, how you told them, and whether you can prove it.

From 18 March 2027, UK banks must send the FCA and PRA an initial incident report, generally within 24 hours of deciding an incident crosses the threshold. Singapore has asked for a call within one hour since 2014. The EU's DORA wants four hours from classification. American banks have 36. The outage itself is rarely the failure that gets fined. The failure is a slow, confused first hour that turns a bad night into a public story.

The TSB lesson

In spring 2018, TSB moved about 5 million customers onto a new IT platform as part of its separation from Lloyds. Millions were locked out of their accounts across online, mobile, phone and branch. Problems lasted for weeks, and the bank did not return to business as usual until December.

Read the findings closely and the technology is not the headline. The regulators pointed to a migration programme that was not properly organised or controlled, weak control of a critical third party supplier, and risk management that was not up to the job. One line matters most for anyone who runs incidents: TSB's early public response understated the scale of the problem.

Outages are forgiven far more easily than confusion about them.When nobody can say what broke, who is fixing it and how many customers are affected, every later message carries less trust.

The clocks that start when production breaks

A bank that serves customers in more than one market now runs several regulatory clocks at once, each with its own trigger and its own first deadline.

The clocks start at different moments. MAS counts from discovery. The UK counts from your decision that the threshold is met. DORA counts from classification but caps it at 24 hours from detection. A good record of exactly when each decision was made is no longer paperwork. It is your evidence.

Where the first hour actually goes

Ask any bank's on call engineers where the first hour of a major outage went, and the answer is rarely "fixing it". It goes in four places.

Finding the right personThe person who knows the payments service may be the fifth to wake up.
Agreeing what brokeThirty minutes proving it is not the network, then not the database.
Getting permissionThe rollback waits for an approver nobody can reach.
Writing it downNobody keeps a clean timeline, and the regulator will ask for one.

A first hour checklist for banks

  1. Page one owner, not a crowd. Escalate only if the first person does not answer, and respect quiet hours for everyone else.
  2. Group the noise. Four hundred alerts from one failure should arrive as one incident.
  3. Put the evidence in one place, so the cause is argued from data in minutes.
  4. Make approvals real and fast, from a phone, with strong authentication and a record.
  5. Keep private incidents private. No details in pages for fraud or security cases.
  6. Log every decision with a timestamp. These are the moments each clock starts from.
  7. Draft the regulator report from the record, and practise on your last serious outage.

The real deadline is the first hour

The new UK rules give banks until 18 March 2027. The work is not filling in a new form. It is making sure that when production breaks at 03:12, the right person is awake in two minutes, the cause is on screen in ten, the fix is approved properly, and every decision is already written down.
